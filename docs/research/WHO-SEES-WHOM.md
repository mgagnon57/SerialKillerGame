# Who sees whom

Compiled 2026-08-03. **This is synthesis, not new fieldwork.** Almost every number in it is already
somewhere in `docs/research/`; what is new is putting them together, because the game's central
mechanic is not the town's shape or its calendar — it is **who is out, who is looking, and who
would notice.**

Where something is inference rather than record, it says so.

---

## 1. The town is empty on a weekday, and this is the biggest fact in the file

Rossville has **no industry of its own**. The economy research searched hard for an operating
elevator, cannery, brickyard or manufacturer in the village during the 1990s and found none — the
industrial base died decades earlier. What the census reports as residents' employment (retail,
manufacturing, health care) is **where residents work**, not what is in the village. Mean travel to
work is **23.6 minutes**, and Rossville sits almost exactly between Danville (15 mi south) and
Hoopeston (9 mi north).

> **So a large share of working-age adults leave the map every weekday morning and come back at
> night.** Rossville is a bedroom village for a corridor, and has been since the industry went.

Set against the age structure — **23.6% under 18, 20.1% over 65** — that leaves a specific daytime
population:

| out of town, ~07:00–17:30 | still here all day |
|---|---|
| most working-age adults | **the retired** — a fifth of the town |
| high-schoolers, once it is term (until 2006) | **children under school age** |
| | the few who work *in* town: four police, school staff, shop and post-office staff, whoever is minding the antique shops |
| | **farmers**, who are in the fields rather than in the town |
| | anyone not working — and the assessed-value data says there is a bottom decile here |

**The consequence for the game is sharp.** Between about eight in the morning and half past five on
a weekday, the people who could witness anything in Rossville are overwhelmingly **the old and the
very young**. That is not a quirk; it is the single most exploitable fact about this town, in both
directions — it is why something could happen unseen, and it is why the one person who *did* see it
is eighty-one and sits at a front window all day.

*Inference from sourced parts: the commute figure and the age split are recorded; the daytime
composition follows from them and is not separately measured.*

## 2. October is the month of least visibility

Three findings from three unrelated files converge, and none of them says this on its own.

**From `THE-YEAR.md`:**
- Corn stands at full height — a **~2.5 m visual barrier** — from July until it is cut, and at the
  start of October **only a third of it has been**. Half the map is opaque at eye level.
- Beans are cut first, so by mid-October the landscape is a **checkerboard of bare stubble and
  standing corn**: cover that is patchy, close to town, and changes weekly.
- Sunset on 1 October is **18:35** and by 1 November **16:49**. The dark arrives an hour and three
  quarters earlier across the month.

**From `WHAT-IT-SOUNDS-LIKE.md`:**
- **Grain dryers run continuously through October, day and night, at 76–106 dBA.** For a few weeks
  the town has a noise floor it does not have the rest of the year.
- Combines and grain trucks are on the roads from late September to mid-November, and because
  harvest runs into the shortening half of the year, **machinery moves on the roads after dark**.
  A vehicle out on a back road at ten at night in October is the least remarkable thing in the
  county.

**From `POLICE-AND-INCIDENT.md`:**
- Four officers. Five arrests a year.

> **October is the month when the fields still hide you, the dark comes early, the noise covers
> everything, and nobody thinks twice about traffic at night.** By December the fields are bare and
> flat to the horizon, and a single set of headlights on a county road is visible for two miles.

The inverse is worth stating too: **June is the worst month to go unseen.** Short crops, light
until half past eight, no machinery, windows open, and everybody outdoors.

## 3. The alleys are the second network

The parallel session, working from the 1940 aerial, established that Rossville has **mid-block
alleys** — a bright line running exactly halfway between each pair of streets, on a block about
117 m deep, so an alley every ~58 m. Their own note on it is the right one: *"where a person can
cross a block without using a street."*

Layer the rest of the record on top and the alley is not a shortcut, it is a **parallel town**:

- **Houses sit at the FRONT of the lot** (`RESIDENTIAL-1913.md`), pushed to the street with the
  depth left open behind. So every house faces the street and turns its back to the alley.
- **There is an outbuilding at the BACK of almost every lot** — **0.84 per house**, in two size
  classes, and *the outbuilding belongs to the lot rather than the house*, so vacant lots carry them
  too. The alley is lined with sheds and garages.
- **17% of residential lots are unbuilt**, at the same median size as the built ones — gaps in the
  fabric, scattered rather than at the edge.
- **30% of parcels are absentee-owned.** A third of the doors have nobody behind them who would know
  what is normal in that alley.

**Streets are overlooked; alleys are not.** A town where every front window faces a street and every
back gate faces a lane has two circulation networks with completely different observation
properties — and only one of them is in the current road generator.

## 4. The moments when the whole town is in one place

The flip side. A village of ~550 households has a small number of events that assemble it, and
those are the moments when absence is noticed.

| when | what | who |
|---|---|---|
| twice a school day | **the school run** to 350 N Chicago St — on the main road | every family with children, twice |
| Sunday morning | **four congregations** — Presbyterian, Methodist, Christian, United Brethren | the churchgoing half |
| Friday nights, in season | **Bobcats basketball** — until 2006 | a meaningful fraction of 1,300 |
| daily | **the post office** | in a town this size, most of it, most weeks |
| any hour, several times a month | **the fire siren** — a volunteer department | ~20 men drop what they are doing, publicly |

That last one is worth dwelling on. **A volunteer fire department is a cross-cutting roster**: it
takes men from every street and every trade, summons them audibly at any hour, and puts them on the
road in a hurry with a public reason. It is the one institution that legitimately explains a person
leaving the house at three in the morning — and the one that would notice if somebody who always
turns out, didn't. *(That Rossville's siren works this way is the ordinary pattern and is
**unconfirmed** for Rossville specifically — see `WHAT-IT-SOUNDS-LIKE.md`.)*

**And the school closing in 2006 removes the biggest one.** After that there is no Friday night and
no local school run for teenagers — the town loses its densest recurring assembly inside the story's
window.

## 5. How news travels, and how that changes across fifteen years

The game opens in **1991** and runs to about **2006**, and the information network is not the same
at both ends.

**1991.** Landlines only. To know something you are told in person, telephoned, or you read it in a
paper out of Danville. **To check on somebody, you go there.** There is no way to reach a person who
is not at home, and no record that you tried. An unanswered phone means nothing.

**By 2006.** Mobile phones are ordinary. A person is reachable away from home, an unanswered call
starts to *mean* something, and there is a log of who called whom.

That shift is a genuine fifteen-year arc and it runs the wrong way for anybody with something to
hide. **The same disappearance is a different event in 1991 and in 2006.**

*Weak sourcing, flagged: I could not establish when cellular coverage actually reached this part of
downstate Illinois. The national picture — 5 million US subscribers by 1990, towers going up around
mid-sized communities through the decade, analog still recommended rurally for coverage — says
rural east-central Illinois was late and patchy, but no source pins Vermilion County. **Treat the
direction as sound and the dates as unresearched.***

## 6. Where the town does not look

Assembled from the record, as places that are opaque, empty, or simply never visited:

- **The North Fork corridor**, west side — the 1940 aerial shows it wide, wooded and dark, with old
  oxbows in it. The only substantial woodland on the map.
- **Standing corn**, July to late October, over most of the country half of the map.
- **Osage orange hedgerows** between fields — ragged lines of thorny growth, visible in the aerial
  as thin dark lines. Field boundaries you cannot see through.
- **Farmsteads inside shelterbelt clumps** — each one a screened enclosure in open ground, and the
  1940 and 2019 photographs show the same arrangement eighty years apart.
- **226 outbuildings** behind the houses, and some on lots with no house at all.
- **The vacant lots** — 17% of residential parcels, scattered through the fabric.
- **Whatever the fire leaves**, from February 2004: a cleared corner, exposed party walls, and
  buildings too damaged to save standing until they were demolished.

And the one that is not a place: **four officers and five arrests a year.** A department that size
does not patrol, it responds. `POLICE-AND-INCIDENT.md` has the rest.

## What is genuinely unresearched

- **Real daily anchors.** School start and end times, church service times, when the post office
  opens, Illinois tavern hours, shift times at the Danville and Hoopeston plants people commuted to.
  `DayPlanner` is inventing these now. They are gettable — mostly from the school district, the
  churches, and the Illinois liquor code — and they would sharpen every hour of the simulated day.
- **When cellular actually arrived here**, per §5.
- **Whether the fire siren, noon whistle and weekly tornado test exist in Rossville**, per §4.
- **What closed in the village itself during the 1990s** — the standing gap from
  `THE-TRAJECTORY.md`, and still the one the local paper would fill.

All four are the kind of thing the historical society could answer in a single phone call, and no
amount of further searching from here will.
